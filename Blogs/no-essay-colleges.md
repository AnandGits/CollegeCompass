# You Don't Have to Write an Essay to Get Into a Good College

*See also: [The UC Personal Insight Questions](uc-piq-guide.md) | [The Common App Essay](common-app-essay-guide.md) | [Essay FAQ](essay-faq.md)*

We've written a fair amount about how to write college essays well. This post is for students who have read all of that, weighed it, and decided they'd rather not.

That is a legitimate choice, and it is not the same thing as giving up. There are hundreds of colleges — including some very good ones — that admit students on transcript and numbers alone. Some of them will admit your student faster, cheaper, and with more scholarship money than schools that require six supplemental essays. A student who fills a list with these schools has not settled. They have opted out of a process that, for their particular situation, wasn't buying them much.

Here's how that works and where the tradeoffs actually are.

## Why some colleges skip the essay

Three reasons, and knowing which one applies tells you a lot about how the school will evaluate your student.

**Formula-based admission.** Many public universities use a selection index — some combination of GPA, course completion, class rank, and where still applicable, test scores. Clear the threshold, you're admitted. The school's position is that an essay wouldn't tell them anything the index doesn't. These schools often set different thresholds for in-state and out-of-state applicants, with out-of-state usually higher.

**Volume and resources.** Reading tens of thousands of essays is expensive. Some large publics and some small colleges alike decide the information isn't worth the cost. These schools frequently require essays anyway for their most competitive programs — engineering and nursing come up constantly — and for scholarships.

**Deliberate friction reduction.** Some colleges make applying easy on purpose, to widen their pool. That includes attracting strong students who will use them as a safety and don't want to write four more essays to do it. You are the intended audience. Use it.

## The California picture

**The CSU system is the single most important fact here.** Twenty-three campuses, no essay required, admission driven primarily by an eligibility index built on GPA and a-g course completion. San Diego State, Cal Poly SLO, San Jose State, Long Beach, Fullerton, Chico — these are not consolation prizes. Cal Poly SLO and SDSU are more selective than many private schools that demand three supplements.

For a Bay Area student, several CSUs are within driving distance, in-state tuition applies, and the entire application requires no personal writing at all.

**UC is the opposite.** All nine undergraduate campuses require four Personal Insight Questions, and there's no way around it. If UC is on the list, your student is writing.

So the practical shape of a California list often looks like this: CSU campuses require no essays, UC requires four PIQs total across all campuses applied to, and private schools require a personal statement plus supplements each. The marginal cost of adding another CSU is near zero. The marginal cost of adding another private is a supplement or two.

That asymmetry is worth stating plainly to a student who's overwhelmed. Adding six CSU campuses to a list costs application fees and about an hour of data entry.

## Out-of-state schools worth knowing about

A number of strong public universities outside California admit on formula and require no essay. As of the most recent survey we've seen, that group included the **University of Alabama**, **Arizona State**, **Northern Arizona**, **University of Arizona**, **University of Arkansas**, **Iowa State**, **University of Iowa**, **Kansas State**, **University of Kansas**, Michigan State-adjacent regionals like **Eastern** and **Western Michigan**, **University of Minnesota Twin Cities**, **University of Missouri**, **Ole Miss**, **Mississippi State**, **University of Nebraska–Lincoln**, **University of Nevada Reno**, **University of New Mexico**, **University of Utah**, **Utah State**, **Washington State**, **West Virginia**, and **University of Wyoming**. Several **CUNY** campuses treat the essay as optional. **Clemson** and **University of South Carolina** list it as optional or honors-only.

We covered several of these in our earlier post on rolling admission and formula-based selection — they pair naturally with this strategy, because rolling admission plus no essay means a student can have an acceptance in hand by October.

Two honest caveats about that list. First, it's assembled from a source last updated in 2024, and admission policies change every cycle. Second, and more importantly:

## The test-score problem nobody flags

Most of these formula schools built their indices around GPA *and* test scores. Since 2020, the landscape has scrambled. CSU is now permanently test-blind — it will not look at SAT or ACT at all. Many other schools went test-optional and some have reinstated requirements.

This matters enormously for a student pursuing this strategy, and it cuts both ways.

**If your student has a strong SAT or ACT score,** formula schools are where that score does the most work. At a holistic-review private, a 1450 is one input among many. At a school with a published index, it may be the difference between automatic admission with a scholarship and a comprehensive review.

**If your student is test-blind by circumstance or choice,** the formula shifts entirely onto GPA and course rigor — which means the transcript is doing all the work, and there's no essay to add context.

Before your student commits to a no-essay list, check each school's current index and what it actually uses. A 2024 list will not reliably tell you.

## When you should write the essay anyway

Being honest about the limits of this strategy:

**Selective schools require essays. All of them.** The most highly ranked no-essay school PrepScholar could identify was the University of Pittsburgh — genuinely good, and also the ceiling. If your student wants a school admitting under about 25%, they are writing essays. There is no workaround.

**Scholarships usually require essays.** This is the most expensive place to opt out. A school may admit your student on numbers and still gate its competitive merit awards behind a written application. At several schools on the list above, the essay is required *only* for scholarship consideration — which means skipping it can cost real money. If your student is in range for merit aid, write the scholarship essay even if the admission application doesn't ask for one.

**Honors colleges require essays.** Same pattern. University of Utah and University of South Carolina, among others, require essays only for honors admission.

**Impacted and competitive majors often require essays.** Engineering, nursing, and business are the usual suspects. A no-essay school may still require writing for the program your student actually wants.

**Special circumstances need explaining.** If there's a semester where grades dropped because of illness, a family situation, or a school change, an essay-free application gives your student no place to say so. A formula sees the dip and nothing else. This is the strongest single argument for writing something, even somewhere that doesn't ask.

## A middle path most students miss

The choice isn't between writing fifteen essays and writing zero.

The Common App personal statement is *one* essay, reusable at more than a thousand schools. The Coalition App works similarly at 130+. The real burden in a heavy application season isn't the personal statement — it's the supplements, which multiply with every private school added.

So a workable compromise: write one good personal statement. Apply to CSU campuses (no essay), several out-of-state formula schools (no essay), and a small number of Common App schools where the personal statement carries most of the weight. Skip or minimize the schools with three-supplement requirements unless your student genuinely wants them.

That's a full, healthy list with a fraction of the writing.

And a note on volume: many required essays are shorter than students imagine. Supplements are commonly 250 words. That's about three paragraphs — less than this section of this post.

## What we'd actually tell a student

If writing essays is causing genuine distress, or if the schools your student wants don't require them, build the list around no-essay schools and feel fine about it. CSU alone can carry a strong California list.

But don't let the essay requirement, by itself, remove a school your student actually wants to attend. Two essays is a weekend. Four years is four years.

The students who regret this decision are almost never the ones who skipped essays at schools they were lukewarm about. They're the ones who skipped a school they cared about because November felt hard.

## Before you apply: a verification checklist

Every claim in this post has a shelf life. For each school on your student's list, confirm directly on the school's admissions page:

- Is an essay required, optional, or not accepted?
- Does the index use test scores, and is the school test-required, optional, or blind this cycle?
- Are in-state and out-of-state thresholds different?
- Does the intended major have separate requirements?
- Does scholarship consideration require an essay?
- Does honors admission require an essay?
- What's the deadline, and is admission rolling?

Search "[School Name] freshman admission requirements" and read the school's own page. Third-party lists — including ours — go stale.
