# The Common App Essay: What Changes When You Leave the UC System

*Part 2 of 2. See also: [The UC Personal Insight Questions](uc-piq-guide.md)*

Most California families apply to both. Their student writes four UC Personal Insight Questions and a Common App personal statement, plus supplements for every private and out-of-state school on the list. It is a lot of writing, and the instinct to reuse material is correct — but only in one direction, and only for certain pieces.

This post covers what the Common App asks for, how it differs from the UC application, and how to sequence the work so your student writes once and adapts rather than starting over four times.

## What the personal statement is for

The Common App personal statement runs up to 650 words and goes to every school your student applies to through the platform. It is the one piece of writing that isn't tailored to anyone. Students choose from seven prompts, and the Common App is accepted at more than a thousand schools.

A smaller alternative exists: the Coalition Application, accepted at more than 130 schools, offering six prompts and asking for 500 to 650 words. Most California families won't need it, but check each school on your list — a few accept one platform and not the other, and a student who assumes Common App covers everything can get caught late.

School-specific supplements are typically around 250 words, though they range from a sentence to a full-length essay.

College Board's summary of what admissions readers look for is a good baseline: command of basic writing, a direct answer to the question asked, an opening that earns attention, a coherent argument or narrative that stays on point, a style that's comfortable for the writer, clean mechanics, accurate facts, and appropriate length.

Notice how much of that is competence rather than brilliance. This matters for calibration. As College Board puts it, an essay will rarely take an applicant out of consideration — but it can elevate one in committee. That's the realistic frame: the personal statement is a differentiator among comparable applicants, not a rescue mission and not a lottery ticket.

## How much does it actually matter?

It depends almost entirely on selectivity, and families consistently overestimate this.

Most American colleges admit a majority of their applicants. At those schools the essay is a modest factor. At highly selective schools it matters considerably more — roughly a quarter of top-tier institutions place considerable importance on it, with another 30% or so assigning it moderate importance. The reason is straightforward: those schools have far more qualified applicants than seats, and the essay is one of the few places a reader can distinguish between two students with near-identical transcripts.

Test-optional and test-blind policies are widely said to push this further, on the logic that fewer data points means the remaining ones carry more weight.

Worth knowing that admissions offices don't uniformly agree. An associate dean of admission at Hamilton College has said directly that going test-optional did not change how they use essays, and that the essay is not a substitute for test scores — academic preparation for their classroom remains the center of the review. That's a useful corrective. The "essays matter more now" claim comes mostly from counselors and journalists rather than from admissions offices, and each school sets its own priorities.

The practical takeaway is unchanged: the transcript is still the largest single factor almost everywhere. An essay strategy that assumes the essay compensates for course rigor is built on sand.

The corollary is one families miss: **if your student's list is mostly schools admitting above 50%, the essay deserves care but not agony.** Time is better spent elsewhere. Anxiety about the essay should scale with the list.

## Supplements are not an afterthought

This is the most common misallocation of effort we see. Families pour weeks into the personal statement and dash off supplements in the last week of December.

At selective schools, supplements can matter as much as the personal statement and sometimes more — UPenn has indicated its "Why Us" essay generally carries more weight than the Common App essay. That makes sense structurally. The personal statement is identical everywhere; the supplement is the only place a student demonstrates they know and want *this* school.

Budget accordingly. A student applying to eight private schools may write 15 or more supplemental responses. That's more total words than the personal statement by a wide margin.

And a mechanical warning that sounds too obvious to state until it happens: do not paste a "Why Columbia" essay into a Penn application. Every year, students submit supplements naming the wrong school. Some of these were otherwise strong applications.

## What carries over from the UC work — and what doesn't

**Carries over cleanly:** the raw material. Topics, experiences, the reflection your student did to identify what matters to them. If they've done honest work generating PIQ topics, the personal statement material is usually already in that pile.

**Carries over in one direction:** length. Compressing a 650-word personal statement into a 350-word PIQ is often workable. Expanding a PIQ into a personal statement usually produces padding. If your student is doing both, **draft long first**, then cut. It's the more efficient sequence and it produces better short pieces.

**Does not carry over:** several things that actively mislead.

*The hook.* Standard advice says open with something that seizes attention — a bold statement, a question, a vivid scene. At 650 words that's affordable. At 350 it isn't. Advice written for one form will hurt the other.

*The "don't repeat your application" rule.* For the Common App personal statement this holds: tell readers something the rest of the file doesn't. For UC PIQs it partly inverts — responses should connect back to activities already on the list, adding depth rather than new territory.

*The rubric.* UC publishes 13 points of comprehensive review, and PIQ responses benefit from mapping to them. The Common App has no equivalent. Chasing a rubric that doesn't exist produces stiff writing.

*The audience structure.* UC readers see four responses in prompt-number order and evaluate the set. Common App readers see one statement plus school-specific supplements. The portfolio logic that governs PIQ selection doesn't apply.

## Topics: the one place good sources disagree

Harvard's admissions-adjacent guidance argues that well-worn themes — the sports obstacle, the immigration story, the volunteer trip, the move — are common precisely because they represent most people's lives coming out of high school, and that taking a fresh approach matters more than avoiding the topic.

College Essay Guy argues close to the opposite: a common topic puts your student into an unintentional curve, competing against many essays on the same subject, some of which will be better. A less common topic has fewer comparisons.

Both are defensible, and the resolution is practical. Topic novelty is a hedge against ordinary execution. Excellent execution makes it irrelevant. If your student is a strong writer with real insight into a common experience, let them write it. If they're a competent but unremarkable writer — which describes most seventeen-year-olds — an unusual angle does more work.

What both sources agree on, and what matters more than topic selection: the resume essay fails. Listing accomplishments, however impressive, tells a reader nothing they can't get from the activities section.

## Five myths worth naming for your student

**"The essay should tell them everything about me."** It can't. 650 words supports one story or one clear through-line. Pick the detail that reveals the most and let the rest of the application do its job.

**"Bigger vocabulary sounds smarter."** It sounds like a thesaurus. Readers can tell when writing isn't in a student's own register, and the mismatch reads as insecurity rather than sophistication.

**"I need to be shocking to stand out."** Gimmicks — a poem instead of an essay, forced humor, uncomfortably intimate disclosure, an unusual format — do make an essay memorable, reliably for the wrong reasons.

**"A great essay is my ticket in."** It's one component. An outstanding essay won't offset weak grades or recommendations, and a merely good one won't undo strong ones.

**"I can reuse supplements."** Sometimes, for genuinely parallel prompts like an activity description. Not for "Why Us" essays, which are the entire point of the supplement.

## A working sequence for a student doing both

1. **Summer, early.** Build the UC Activities List first, even if UC isn't the priority. It's the best inventory of raw material either application will use.
2. **Summer, mid.** Generate topics from self-knowledge, not from prompts. What does your student care about, what have they actually done, what would a close friend say about them?
3. **Summer, late.** Draft the personal statement long — 650 words or more. This is your deepest piece of writing and everything else borrows from it.
4. **August–September.** Map topics to the four UC prompts. Audit the set for redundancy and coverage. Compress where the personal statement material fits.
5. **October–November.** Supplements. Research each school properly; "Why Us" essays fail on generic praise. Note that early decision and early action deadlines can fall as early as November — if your student is applying early anywhere, the whole schedule above moves up by roughly a month, and it collides directly with the UC filing window.
6. **Throughout.** Set drafts aside for days at a time. Read them aloud — it catches clumsy and false sentences faster than any other method. Have readers who don't all belong to the same demographic, and when someone says they're confused, believe them.

## The one thing to tell an anxious student

Go beyond the what and the who into the how and the why. A trip is not a topic; what the trip changed is. A person you admire is not a topic; what they did for you and why it mattered is.

That single instruction fixes more weak drafts than any other piece of advice in this post.
