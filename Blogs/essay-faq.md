# College Essay FAQ

*Companion to [The UC Personal Insight Questions](uc-piq-guide.md) and [The Common App Essay](common-app-essay-guide.md)*

## Getting oriented

**How many essays will my student actually write?**

For a typical California list of four UC campuses and six private schools: four PIQs at 350 words, one personal statement at 650 words, and anywhere from six to twenty supplemental responses ranging from 50 to 650 words each. The supplements are usually the largest total, and families almost always underestimate them.

**Does the UC application have a personal statement?**

No. UC uses eight Personal Insight Questions, of which students answer four at up to 350 words each. There is no supplement and no separate essay. The same four responses go to every UC campus your student applies to.

**Can we submit different PIQ responses to different UC campuses?**

No. One application, one set of four responses, all campuses.

**Do UC campuses read the PIQs differently?**

Each campus conducts its own review, so emphasis can vary, but all use the same 13 points of comprehensive review and the same four responses.

**When does the UC application open and close?**

It opens August 1 and the filing period runs through November 30. There is no early action and no rolling admission — submitting early confers no advantage in the review.

## How much this matters

**How important is the essay, really?**

It scales with selectivity. Most American colleges admit a majority of applicants, and at those schools the essay is a modest factor. At highly selective schools roughly a quarter assign it considerable importance and another 30% or so moderate importance. If your student's list is mostly schools admitting above 50%, the essay deserves care but not agony.

**Can a great essay get my student into a school they aren't otherwise competitive for?**

No. It is one component among several. An outstanding essay won't offset weak grades or recommendations. What it can do is distinguish your student from similarly qualified applicants — which, at selective schools, is often the actual decision being made.

**Can a bad essay keep them out?**

Rarely on its own. An essay will seldom remove an applicant from consideration, though it can fail to help. The exceptions are essays with serious mechanical problems or content that raises real concerns.

**Do essays matter more now that many schools are test-optional?**

You'll hear this asserted confidently, and it's contested. The logic is reasonable — fewer data points means the remaining ones carry more weight — and it's clearly true at test-blind schools, including the entire UC system.

But admissions offices don't uniformly endorse it. An associate dean of admission at Hamilton College has stated that test-optional policy did not change how they use essays, and that essays don't substitute for scores; academic preparation stays at the center of their review. Each school sets its own priorities, and the "essays matter more now" line comes largely from counselors and the press rather than from admissions offices themselves.

Plan as though the transcript is still the largest factor, because at most schools it is.

## Choosing topics

**Should my student avoid common topics?**

Sources disagree honestly on this. One view holds that common themes are common because they describe most teenagers' lives, and a fresh angle matters more than the topic. Another holds that a common topic puts you in competition with many similar essays. Our practical read: topic novelty is a hedge against ordinary writing. A strong writer with real insight can write about anything. Most students benefit from an unusual angle.

**What topics genuinely tend to fall flat?**

The resume essay that lists accomplishments. The big game won or lost with a life lesson attached. The sports injury and recovery. The service trip abroad that taught gratitude. The performance where nerves were overcome. None are forbidden — all are crowded.

**For UC prompt 5, what challenges work?**

Substantive ones: circumstances requiring real responsibility, sustained obstacles, situations with genuine stakes. Topics that tend to underperform include breakups, not making a team, a single difficult class, and the shy-student-finds-their-voice arc. A poor grade belongs in the Academic History section rather than a PIQ.

**Should my student write about mental health, family difficulty, or trauma?**

It can work, and some of the strongest PIQ responses do exactly this. Two tests: can your student write about it with enough distance to reflect rather than relive, and does the response spend most of its length on what they did and learned rather than on the difficulty itself? If either answer is no, choose something else. Nobody is obligated to disclose anything.

**Can they write about a job?**

Yes, and it's underused. Work — especially work supporting a family — demonstrates responsibility, leadership, and time management better than most extracurriculars. It fits several UC prompts.

**What if my student "hasn't done anything special"?**

Most haven't, by the standard they're imagining. Readers are not looking for exceptional experiences; they're looking for the thinking a student does about ordinary ones. A UC community-contribution response can be about something as small as consistently cleaning up after others.

## Writing and structure

**Should the essay open with a dramatic hook?**

Depends on the form. At 650 words on the Common App, yes — a vivid opening earns its space. At 350 words on a PIQ, a long hook is expensive; name the subject in the first line or two and spend the rest on evidence and insight. Advice written for one form frequently misleads on the other.

**What structure should we use?**

Two cover almost everything. **Narrative** for challenge-based material: challenge, what you did, what you learned, roughly a third each. **Montage** for material spanning several experiences: brief opening, then the word budget divided across examples. Fewer examples means more depth; more means more range.

**What does "show, don't tell" actually mean here?**

Give readers evidence that leads them to a conclusion instead of stating the conclusion. "I'm a leader" is telling. Describing what you did when two people on your team stopped speaking is showing.

**How do we know if it's specific enough?**

Read it and ask whether you can picture it. If not, the writing is operating at the level of claims rather than examples.

**Does the whole thing need to be one story?**

No. Montage works well, particularly for the creativity and academic-subject prompts. What it does need is a through-line.

**How long should a "short answer" supplement be?**

Close to the limit. A 250-word prompt answered in 80 words reads as indifference toward that school.

## Revision

**How many drafts?**

Enough that your student has been away from it. Revision requires distance more than it requires time — a draft written in July and reread in September reveals its problems immediately.

**What's the single most effective revision technique?**

Reading it aloud. It exposes sentences that are clumsy, overlong, or false faster than silent rereading.

**Who should read it?**

People who don't all resemble each other — a teacher, a parent, a younger sibling. Ask each what they took away. When a reader says they're confused, believe them and revise, rather than explaining what you meant.

**Should we hire someone?**

Optional, and the value depends on the list. What matters more than credentials is whether the person makes the writing sound more like your student or less. If drafts start coming back sounding like a thirty-five-year-old, that's a problem regardless of who's doing the editing.

## Parents

**How involved should I be?**

Read drafts, ask questions, give reactions. Don't write sentences. A parent's instinct is toward polish and impressiveness; both work against a seventeen-year-old's voice, and readers notice.

**My student's essay sounds too simple. Should I push them to elevate it?**

Almost certainly not. Vocabulary a student wouldn't use in conversation reads as insecure rather than sophisticated. Clear, direct, simple language is what admissions offices explicitly ask for.

**My student wants to write about something that makes me uncomfortable. What do I do?**

Ask whether the response spends most of its length on reflection and action rather than on the difficult material itself, and whether they've had enough distance from it. If both are yes, it's likely a stronger essay than the safe alternative. If you're uncomfortable because it's revealing rather than because it's risky, that discomfort is not a reason to intervene.

## Logistics

**What's the Coalition Application, and do we need it?**

A second application platform, accepted at more than 130 schools, with six prompts and a 500-to-650-word range. The Common App is accepted at over a thousand schools and covers most lists. Check each school individually — a few accept one and not the other. If both are options for a school, use whichever your student has already drafted for; the essays are close enough in length to adapt.

**How long are supplements?**

Typically around 250 words, though the range runs from a single sentence to a full-length essay. Check every prompt individually rather than assuming.

**Do word limits matter?**

Yes. Platforms enforce hard limits. Where the limit is a recommendation, going substantially over signals that you didn't edit.

**Can supplements be reused across schools?**

Sometimes for genuinely parallel prompts — an activity description, for example. Not for "Why Us" essays, which exist specifically to test whether you know this school. And check every one for the wrong school name before submitting. This error is more common than you'd think.

**Are UC PIQs checked for plagiarism?**

Yes. Submissions are screened, and copied material can flag an application.

**When should we start?**

Summer before senior year is the standard answer and it's correct, mostly because it's the last stretch with enough slack for real revision. If your student is reading this in October, start now and prioritize: personal statement first if the list is private-school-heavy, PIQs first if it's UC-heavy, supplements for reach schools before supplements for likelies.
